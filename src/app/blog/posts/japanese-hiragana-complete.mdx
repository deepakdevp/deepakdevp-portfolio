# ひらがな Complete! 🎉

Two weeks ago, I looked at hiragana and saw squiggles. Today, I can read full sentences.

This is a small milestone — there's still katakana, 2,000+ kanji, grammar, and actual fluency ahead. But this one feels special because it's the first time Japanese went from "incomprehensible symbols" to "oh, I can actually read this."

## The Final Push

Last week I was at maybe 70% recognition. I could read most characters but stumbled on:
- **ぬ (nu)** vs **め (me)** — they look almost identical
- **わ (wa)** vs **れ (re)** vs **ね (ne)** — all have similar swoops
- **る (ru)** vs **ろ (ro)** — I mixed these up constantly

My wife (who's N5 level) kept laughing when I read her practice sentences because I'd confidently read the wrong character.

Example:
- What it said: **わたしはねこです** (watashi wa neko desu - I am a cat)
- What I read: **わたしはめこです** (watashi wa meko desu - I am a... meko??)

She'd correct me: "That's め (me), not ね (ne). Look at the stroke direction."

## What Finally Clicked: Writing by Hand

I've been using Anki flashcards, which helped with recognition. But I kept confusing similar characters.

My wife suggested: **"Stop just looking. Start writing."**

So I bought a basic practice notebook and spent 15 minutes every morning writing each character 10 times.

This was a game-changer.

When you write **ぬ (nu)**, you start with a hook on the left, then cross through. When you write **め (me)**, you start at the top and loop down. The *motion* is different, and muscle memory makes it stick.

After three days of handwriting practice, I stopped mixing them up.

## Spaced Repetition is Real

I've been using Anki with a custom hiragana deck. The algorithm is simple: if you get a card right, it shows up less frequently. If you get it wrong, it shows up more often.

Some characters I nailed immediately and now see once a week:
- **あ (a)** - looks like an "a" with a hat
- **か (ka)** - looks like someone cutting with a knife (刀 kana is knife!)
- **し (shi)** - simple swoosh

Others kept coming back daily because I kept messing them up:
- **さ (sa)** vs **ち (chi)** - both have dangly bits
- **そ (so)** vs **ん (n)** - both have a zig-zag
- **り (ri)** vs **い (i)** - is that one stroke or two?

By the end of two weeks, even the tricky ones became automatic.

## Testing Myself: Reading Real Sentences

My wife wrote me practice sentences using only hiragana (no kanji). I had to read them out loud.

**Sentence 1:**
> わたしのなまえはディパクです。

Translation: "My name is Deepak."

I read it slowly: "wa-ta-shi no na-ma-e wa di-pa-ku de-su."

Success! ✅

**Sentence 2:**
> きょうはげつようびです。

Translation: "Today is Monday."

I stumbled on **げつようび** (getsuyoubi - Monday). I knew all the characters but didn't recognize the word.

My wife: "げつ (getsu) is moon, よう (you) is day. Monday is moon-day!"

Mind. Blown. 🌙

Turns out Japanese day names are based on celestial bodies:
- 月曜日 (getsuyoubi) - Monday (moon)
- 火曜日 (kayoubi) - Tuesday (fire)
- 水曜日 (suiyoubi) - Wednesday (water)
- 木曜日 (mokuyoubi) - Thursday (tree/wood)
- 金曜日 (kinyoubi) - Friday (gold/metal)
- 土曜日 (doyoubi) - Saturday (earth/soil)
- 日曜日 (nichiyoubi) - Sunday (sun)

This is way cooler than English (where Thursday is just "Thor's day" and nobody thinks about it).

## Claude Code as a Study Partner

I've been using Claude Code to quiz me on hiragana.

I'd ask: "Give me a random hiragana character and I'll type the romaji."

Claude would respond:
> **Character:** ふ  
> **Your answer?**

I'd type: "fu"

> ✅ Correct! Here's the next one: **ほ**

It's simple but effective. The instant feedback helps, and I can do it anywhere (I quiz myself during coffee breaks at work).

I also asked Claude to generate practice sentences using only hiragana I've learned. This was great for reading practice without hitting kanji I don't know yet.

## The Moment It Felt Real

Last weekend, my wife and I were watching a Japanese cooking show (she loves them for listening practice).

On screen, there was a sign in hiragana: **おいしい** (oishii).

I read it out loud without thinking.

My wife looked at me: "You just read that, didn't you?"

"Wait... yeah, I did. What does it mean?"

"Delicious."

That was the moment it clicked. I wasn't *studying* hiragana anymore. I was *using* it.

## Hiragana in the Wild

Now that I can read hiragana, I see it everywhere:
- YouTube video titles
- Tweets from Japanese accounts I follow
- Japanese restaurants (menus often have furigana - hiragana above kanji)
- Anime subtitles (some shows include hiragana for kids)

It's like suddenly being able to see a hidden layer of the world.

## What's Next: Katakana

Katakana is the next step. It's used for:
- Foreign loanwords (e.g., コンピュータ - konpyuuta - computer)
- Emphasis (like italics in English)
- Onomatopoeia

My wife says katakana is easier because you already know the sounds (same as hiragana), you just need to learn the new shapes.

Some katakana I already recognize from seeing them in anime:
- **ア (a)** - looks like an uppercase "A" missing a stroke
- **カ (ka)** - looks like a claw
- **サ (sa)** - looks like a backwards "E"

I'm using the same strategy: Anki + handwriting + daily practice.

Goal: Finish katakana in one week (since I already know the sounds, just need the shapes).

## Reading vs. Speaking vs. Understanding

One thing I'm realizing: **reading hiragana ≠ understanding Japanese**.

I can read **わたしはがくせいです** (watashi wa gakusei desu) perfectly.

But I had to look up what **がくせい (gakusei)** means (student).

Reading is just the first step. Next comes:
- Vocabulary (learning words)
- Grammar (understanding particles like は, が, を, に)
- Listening (recognizing spoken words)
- Speaking (producing natural-sounding sentences)

But hiragana is the foundation. You can't learn vocabulary without being able to read it.
