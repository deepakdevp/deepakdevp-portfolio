# カタカナ Complete! (Katakana Done!)

One week after finishing hiragana, I've completed katakana.

My wife was right — katakana is easier. You already know the sounds (same as hiragana), you just need to learn 46 new shapes.

But here's what I didn't expect: **katakana unlocks so much more of real-world Japanese** than hiragana did.

## Why Katakana Matters More Than I Thought

Katakana is used for:
1. **Foreign loanwords** (especially English)
2. **Emphasis** (like italics or bold)
3. **Onomatopoeia** (sound effects)
4. **Non-Japanese names**

At first, I thought "loanwords" meant a few random English words sprinkled in. Nope. Modern Japanese is *full* of English loanwords, especially in:
- Technology
- Business
- Food
- Fashion

Example sentence:
> コンピュータでメールをチェックします。

Translation: "I check email on a computer."

- コンピュータ (konpyuuta) = computer
- メール (meeru) = email (from "mail")
- チェック (chekku) = check

Half the sentence is English words written in katakana!

## Learning Katakana: Same Strategy, Faster

I used the exact same approach as hiragana:
- **Anki flashcards** for recognition
- **Handwriting practice** for muscle memory
- **Real-world examples** to make it stick

But since I already knew the sounds, I could focus entirely on recognizing shapes.

Day 1: Learned all 46 characters (just looked at them, didn't memorize)  
Day 2-4: Anki drills + handwriting practice  
Day 5-7: Reading real katakana words

Total time: ~30 min/day for one week.

## Characters That Tripped Me Up

Some katakana characters look similar and I kept mixing them up:

### シ (shi) vs ツ (tsu)
These two are *almost identical*. The only difference is stroke angle:
- **シ** has vertical strokes (like "|||")
- **ツ** has horizontal strokes (like "—")

My wife's tip: "Think of シ (shi) as 'shhhh' (vertical like a person standing). Think of ツ (tsu) as a smile (horizontal)."

After that, I stopped mixing them up.

### ソ (so) vs ン (n)
Same problem — they look almost the same:
- **ソ** has a steeper angle
- **ン** is more slanted

I still get these wrong sometimes, but context helps. If a word ends with ン, it's probably **n** (most Japanese words don't end with "so").

### ア (a) vs マ (ma)
Both have a similar structure:
- **ア** looks like a capital "A" without the middle line
- **マ** has an extra stroke at the bottom

Once I started writing them, the muscle memory made it obvious.

## Katakana Pronunciation: Not Quite English

One tricky thing: **katakana words aren't pronounced exactly like English**.

Japanese doesn't have all English sounds, so they approximate:

| English | Katakana | Pronunciation |
|---------|----------|---------------|
| computer | コンピュータ | konpyuuta |
| strike | ストライキ | sutoraiki |
| McDonald's | マクドナルド | makudonarudo |
| Christmas | クリスマス | kurisumasu |

Rules I've learned:
1. **No consonant clusters** — "strike" becomes "su-to-ra-i-ki" (each consonant gets a vowel)
2. **R/L confusion** — Japanese "r" is between English "r" and "l"
3. **Vowel extension** — "コーヒー" (koohii) stretches the "o" sound

This makes listening harder than reading. When I hear "konpyuuta," I have to translate it back to "computer" in my head.

## Katakana for Names

Katakana is also used for non-Japanese names.

My name in katakana: **ディパク** (dipaku)

Breaking it down:
- **ディ** (di) — This is a special combo (デ + small イ)
- **パ** (pa)
- **ク** (ku)

When I introduce myself in Japanese, I'll say:
> わたしの なまえは ディパク です。  
> (Watashi no namae wa Dipaku desu.)  
> My name is Deepak.

My wife's name: **プリヤ** (puriya)

She taught me that katakana names don't always sound exactly like the original. Japanese phonetics limit what sounds are possible, so names get adapted.

## Hiragana vs. Katakana: When to Use What

Now that I know both, here's the pattern:

| Script | Use |
|--------|-----|
| **Hiragana** | Native Japanese words, grammatical particles, verb endings |
| **Katakana** | Foreign loanwords, non-Japanese names, emphasis |
| **Kanji** | Nouns, verb stems, most content words |

Example sentence:
> **私**は **エンジニア** です。**コンピュータ** で **仕事** を します。

- **私** (わたし, watashi) - "I" (kanji + hiragana reading)
- **エンジニア** (enjinia) - "engineer" (katakana loanword)
- **コンピュータ** (konpyuuta) - "computer" (katakana loanword)
- **仕事** (しごと, shigoto) - "work" (kanji)
- です、を、します - grammatical parts (hiragana)

Real Japanese is a mix of all three scripts. That's why you need to learn all of them.
